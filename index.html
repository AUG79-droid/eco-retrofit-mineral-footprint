Learning checkpoint 1

A mineral list is the start, not the answer

1. Electronics create long upstream chains

An avionics unit may be purchased from a Tier-1 supplier, while the metal inside it can pass through traders, processors, smelters/refiners and mines before reaching the component manufacturer. This means the party signing the purchase order may be several commercial layers away from the ecological impact.

2. 3TG has a specific meaning

The EU Conflict Minerals Regulation addresses tin, tantalum, tungsten and gold — 3TG — for in-scope Union importers and uses a due-diligence framework linked to conflict-affected and high-risk areas. It should not be used as shorthand for “all minerals that matter”. Rare earths, cobalt, lithium, nickel and other critical materials can create material environmental, human-rights or resilience risks through other regulatory and policy frameworks.

3. Criticality is not the same as environmental harm

A material may be labelled critical because of economic importance and supply risk. That does not automatically mean every mine has high ecological impact. Conversely, a non-critical material can still be sourced from a project with severe habitat or water impacts. Good decisions keep supply criticality and impact significance as separate analytical dimensions.

4. OECD due diligence is risk-based

The OECD minerals framework uses a five-step structure: establish management systems; identify and assess risk; design and implement a response strategy; support independent third-party audit at identified points; and report on due diligence. The aim is not simply to collect supplier forms. It is to identify significant risk and respond to it.

5. Translate design into an upstream question

The engineer should be able to explain why the material is functionally required, whether alternative technology exists, whether the design creates single-source dependency, what qualification barriers apply and whether lower-impact or secondary material can be introduced without compromising safety. This creates a bridge between ecodesign and responsible sourcing.

6. A practical engineering screen for mineral materiality

When a design team says a mineral is “important”, that can mean several different things and they should not be collapsed into one idea. Functional materiality asks whether the mineral is technically important to the component's performance. Supply materiality asks whether the mineral comes from a concentrated or disruption-prone market. Impact materiality asks whether sourcing that mineral can create serious environmental or human-rights harm upstream. A robust retrofit review checks all three at the same time.

That is why a responsible engineering review should move through five questions: where is the mineral likely to sit in the product architecture, what are the credible upstream sources, what is the regulatory or customer relevance, what environmental or biodiversity red flags exist, and what design levers could reduce exposure in future versions of the product.

Learning checkpoint 2

A certificate is evidence only within its scope

1. Supplier claims need a defined boundary

Statements such as “responsible”, “ethical” or “sustainable” are too broad unless the evidence defines which mineral, product, facility, time period and part of the supply chain were assessed. A mine-site standard and a smelter audit answer different questions. Neither should be presented as universal proof of the complete product chain.

2. Smelter/refiner identification is powerful

For many mineral systems, the smelter or refiner is a practical control point because material from multiple mines is transformed into a standardised metal. Schemes such as the Responsible Minerals Assurance Process can provide useful information at this stage for relevant minerals, but downstream companies still need their own risk-based due diligence.

3. Chain of custody and impact assurance differ

Chain of custody can help show where material came from or how claims move through the supply chain. Environmental assurance asks whether mine operations manage biodiversity, water, waste, closure and other impacts. A company needs to understand which question a document actually answers.

4. IRMA is mine-site focused

The Initiative for Responsible Mining Assurance provides a mine-site standard covering environmental and social topics including biodiversity/ecosystem services, protected areas, water, waste, human rights, labour and mine closure. Its value is not the word “certificate” but the detailed scope and performance information associated with the mine assessment.

5. Corrective action is part of due diligence

Responsible sourcing does not necessarily mean instantly abandoning every supplier when a risk is found. OECD due diligence distinguishes response options based on severity and leverage: continue while implementing risk mitigation, temporarily suspend while pursuing mitigation, or disengage after failed mitigation or where severe risk makes disengagement appropriate. The response should be documented and monitored.

6. Evidence hierarchy: from claim to confidence

Not all evidence has the same decision value. A good training rule is to distinguish five levels. Level 1: promotional claims or one-page certificates with no scope definition. Level 2: supplier policy and questionnaires. Level 3: smelter/refiner and origin disclosure. Level 4: risk assessment, corrective-action records and site-level environmental information. Level 5: recognised independent assurance plus internally reviewed decision logic. The higher the upstream risk, the higher on this ladder the evidence should be.

This prevents a common failure mode in sustainability reviews: a company receives a glossy certificate and mistakes it for a complete conclusion, even though the certificate may say nothing about biodiversity, water, waste, protected areas or chain of custody.

Learning checkpoint 3

Mining changes land long before the metal reaches the aircraft

1. Environmental due diligence now has explicit OECD guidance

The OECD's environmental due-diligence handbook for mineral supply chains expands the established minerals framework to environmental risks. It addresses climate, biodiversity, water, waste and other environmental impacts and is designed to help companies use a risk-based due-diligence process rather than rely on a simple approved/rejected mine list.

2. Biodiversity risk starts with location

Mine screening should consider protected areas, Key Biodiversity Areas or other locally significant habitats, threatened species, ecosystem services, ecological connectivity and cumulative pressure. A mine in an already industrialised landscape can still affect rare habitats or water systems; a remote landscape is not automatically pristine.

3. Footprint extends beyond the pit

Roads, waste-rock areas, tailings storage, power infrastructure, camps and water abstraction can fragment landscapes and alter hydrology. The sourcing decision should therefore consider direct, indirect and cumulative impacts rather than only the visible excavation boundary.

4. The mitigation hierarchy matters

Good environmental management prioritises avoidance first, then minimisation, restoration/rehabilitation and, where appropriate and credible, compensation or offsetting for residual impacts. A supplier that immediately jumps to “offsets” without showing avoidance and minimisation may be masking a weak impact strategy.

5. Ask for performance, not slogans

Useful mine-site evidence can include baseline ecological studies, impact assessments, protected-area screening, water balances, biodiversity action plans, rehabilitation/closure plans, tailings management, incident history, stakeholder grievance information, monitoring results and independent assurance. The precise package should be proportionate to the risk.

6. The landscape around the mine matters as much as the pit

A mine can fragment habitat even when the excavation footprint looks small on a map. Access roads, power corridors, camps, waste facilities, tailings, water abstraction, dust, noise and induced land-use change can all expand the ecological footprint. For high-value ecosystems, the real question is not merely “does the mine exist?” but “what ecological processes does the whole project alter across the surrounding landscape?”

That is why a serious sourcing review asks for buffer-zone mapping, hydrological context, protected-area overlap, cumulative impacts from nearby projects and whether mitigation relies mainly on avoidance, minimisation, restoration or offsets. The earlier this question is asked, the more options the company still has.

Learning checkpoint 4

Different frameworks answer different questions

1. EU Conflict Minerals Regulation

Regulation (EU) 2017/821 establishes supply-chain due-diligence obligations for in-scope Union importers of tin, tantalum, tungsten and gold. It is linked to risks in conflict-affected and high-risk areas and draws on OECD due-diligence principles. It is not a comprehensive biodiversity law and not a list of every mineral relevant to aerospace.

2. Critical Raw Materials Act

The EU Critical Raw Materials Act addresses strategic dependencies, diversification, processing, recycling and supply security for critical and strategic raw materials. Environmental performance and circularity form part of the wider policy context, but “critical” primarily describes economic/supply importance rather than automatically ranking mines by ecological damage.

3. Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence

The EU Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive establishes human-rights and environmental due-diligence obligations for companies within its scope, subject to its phased application and legal requirements. A training app should not imply that every aerospace supplier or mineral transaction is automatically covered; applicability has to be checked.

4. Customer and company requirements can go further

Even where a particular mineral falls outside a specific law, procurement contracts, defence customer expectations, internal responsible-sourcing policies, sustainability risk processes or future reporting requirements can justify deeper evidence. Compliance is a floor, not necessarily the whole decision framework.

5. Avoid regulatory greenwashing

A statement such as “EU compliant, therefore environmentally responsible” is too broad. Legal compliance only supports the specific obligations that apply. Responsible communication should state what was assessed, under which standard, for which suppliers/minerals/sites, and what remains uncertain.

6. Think in regulatory layers, not in one-rule answers

In practice, companies navigate a stack of expectations: formal law, customer requirements, internal standards, contract clauses, assurance frameworks and reputation risk. A mineral stream may be outside one legal scope and still be highly relevant because a defence customer, investor, auditor or procurement policy expects stronger due diligence. The engineering function therefore benefits from a simple habit: ask not only “is it mandatory?” but also “what decision could fail later if we ignore this now?”

This mindset is particularly useful in retrofit and upgrade programmes, where the direct objective is technical performance but the hidden risk may sit in sourcing evidence, supplier change, public scrutiny or future programme reviews.

Learning checkpoint 5

Recycled content is an engineering route, not a marketing adjective

1. Secondary material can reduce primary extraction demand

Using recycled metal can reduce the need for some virgin mineral extraction and may lower certain lifecycle impacts, depending on collection, processing, energy, transport, yield and alloy quality. The exact environmental advantage should be supported by credible data rather than assumed from the word “recycled”.

2. Aerospace qualification still applies

Material chemistry, contamination limits, mechanical/electrical properties, process stability, certification basis and configuration control remain non-negotiable. A sourcing route is not responsible if it reduces mining impact but introduces unacceptable technical risk.

3. Recycled-content claims also need traceability

Companies should understand whether the claim is based on post-consumer, pre-consumer or process scrap, what mass-balance or chain-of-custody method is used, and how the percentage is verified. Otherwise “contains recycled material” can become another unqualified marketing statement.

4. Design can enable future recovery

Modularity, replaceable subassemblies, material declarations, reversible joining and easier separation can improve future recovery. Not every electronics module can be designed for simple closed-loop recycling, but design teams can avoid making disassembly unnecessarily difficult.

5. Circularity and responsible mining are complementary

Even aggressive recycling cannot immediately eliminate demand for primary critical minerals, especially in growing technologies. A mature strategy therefore combines responsible primary sourcing, longer product/component life, repair and reuse, qualified secondary feedstock, efficient material use and improved end-of-life recovery.

6. What engineers should verify before accepting recycled content

Secondary material should be challenged with the same discipline as virgin material. Key questions include: what scrap category is used, how contamination is controlled, whether the recycled fraction is physically segregated or mass-balanced, what analytical testing confirms composition, which process controls protect consistency, whether the route has already been qualified, and what traceability exists from recovered source to final alloy or compound.

Good circular sourcing is therefore not only a sustainability story. It is a data and process-control story. When the evidence is good, recycled content can reduce extraction pressure and improve resilience; when the evidence is weak, it becomes another unsupported claim.

Advanced knowledge base

Mineral sourcing, biodiversity and aerospace design

Use this section as the technical reference behind the game.

1. OECD five-step model

Responsible mineral sourcing is commonly structured around management systems, risk identification/assessment, risk response, independent audit at identified supply-chain points and public reporting. The process is iterative: new information or supplier changes can trigger reassessment.

2. Environmental due diligence

OECD guidance now explicitly helps mineral-chain companies address environmental risks including biodiversity, climate, water and waste. The logic is risk-based and should be integrated into existing minerals due-diligence systems rather than treated as a disconnected environmental questionnaire.

3. High-value habitats

Protected-area status is one signal, but not the only one. Key Biodiversity Areas, threatened-species habitat, ecosystem connectivity, culturally important ecosystems, water-dependent systems and irreplaceable ecological functions may justify heightened scrutiny even outside formally protected boundaries.

4. IRMA

IRMA offers independent mine-site assessment against requirements covering social and environmental responsibility. For environmental review, its standard includes biodiversity/ecosystem services, protected areas, water, waste, tailings and closure among other topics.

5. 3TG

Tin, tantalum, tungsten and gold are the specific minerals covered by the EU Conflict Minerals Regulation for in-scope importers. Do not expand or shrink that legal list casually in training communications.

6. Critical Raw Materials Act

The EU framework seeks secure, resilient and more circular access to strategic and critical raw materials through extraction, processing, recycling and diversification objectives. It is important for aerospace and defence supply resilience, but criticality should not be confused with a mine-level environmental rating.

7. CSDDD

The Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive creates obligations for companies within its legal scope to address certain adverse human-rights and environmental impacts in chains of activities. Applicability, timing and company thresholds must be checked rather than assumed.

8. Supplier certificates

A certificate should be checked for issuing body, scope, site/mineral/product covered, assurance level, assessment date, methodology, exclusions and validity. The label on the front page is less important than the claim actually supported.

9. Mine-site biodiversity

Key evidence includes baseline ecology, impact significance, avoidance areas, habitat conversion, threatened species, water impacts, restoration planning, monitoring and cumulative impacts. An audit score without access to the relevant environmental findings may be insufficient for a high-risk source.

10. Chain of custody

Traceability may use physical segregation, controlled blending, mass balance or documented chain-of-custody systems depending on the material and scheme. Procurement teams should understand the claim model before communicating that a specific aircraft part came from a specific mine.

11. Recycled claims

Recycled content should define the percentage, material boundary, scrap classification, accounting method and verification approach. Recycled-content evidence and responsible primary-sourcing evidence can coexist in the same product chain.

12. Design levers

Engineers can influence upstream demand through material efficiency, longer component life, repairability, modularity, reuse, qualification of secondary materials, substitution and specification choices — while respecting safety, certification and performance constraints.

13. Red flags

Potential red flags include refusal to name smelters/refiners, unexplained changes in origin, mine projects overlapping sensitive areas, serious water/tailings incidents, weak closure planning, assurance claims with unclear scope and supplier statements that substitute values language for evidence.

14. Avoid greenwashing

Do not call a mineral “green”, “zero-impact” or “fully sustainable”. Describe the evidence: traceability coverage, mine assessment, recycled percentage, specific risks reviewed, corrective actions and remaining limitations.

15. Aerospace trade-offs

Responsible sourcing decisions sit inside airworthiness, configuration control, export/security constraints, obsolescence, cost and mission readiness. Sustainability does not remove these requirements. The skill is to integrate them into one transparent decision rather than treating sustainability as a separate afterthought.

16. Governance

A mature process assigns ownership across engineering, procurement, quality, sustainability/environment, compliance and programme management. Escalation criteria, evidence retention and supplier corrective-action responsibilities should be explicit.

17. Smelter/refiner chokepoints

For many metals, smelters and refiners are the most practical control point because they aggregate material from multiple upstream sources. That makes them highly relevant for chain mapping, assurance and corrective action, even though they do not eliminate the need to understand the mine context.

18. Certificates can hide scope gaps

A certificate may validate a management system, a mine site, a product claim, a recycled-content method or a limited audit scope. Unless the user reads the scope carefully, the organisation can mistakenly attribute more confidence to the document than it actually supports.

19. Cumulative impacts

A mine may appear manageable in isolation but still contribute to serious degradation when combined with other mines, roads, industrial plants, water extraction or deforestation in the same landscape. Strong screening therefore looks beyond a single site boundary.

20. The mitigation hierarchy

Environmental decision-making should distinguish between avoidance, minimisation, restoration and offsetting. In high-value ecosystems, the most responsible answer may be to avoid a source rather than to rely on compensation claims after damage occurs.

21. What good corrective action looks like

Good supplier response is time-bound, evidence-based and linked to clear responsibilities. It should define the issue, risk level, required action, timeline, follow-up evidence, escalation path and consequences if improvement does not occur.

22. Greenwashing warning signs

Watch for emotionally attractive language such as “ethical by nature”, “planet-friendly minerals” or “fully green electronics” when the supplier cannot show chain data, impact criteria, verification scope or a record of responding to real upstream risks.

23. Design levers that reduce future mineral pressure

Beyond sourcing, engineering can reduce exposure through design-for-repair, lower material intensity, longer service life, component commonality, easier disassembly, material declarations and design choices that improve future recovery and recycling.

24. Decision quality in retrofit boards

The best sourcing decision is rarely the one with the single best headline metric. Decision quality improves when the board explicitly weighs technical integrity, traceability, ecological safeguards and resilience together, documents trade-offs and records why a decision was accepted.
